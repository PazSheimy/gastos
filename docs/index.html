See where your money goes

Gastos reads the statement file you download from your bank or card and sorts every purchase: groceries, dining, gas, flights and more. It splits personal from business, shows your spending month by month, and makes an Excel report you can keep or send to your accountant. If you travel, it also finds your trips and shows what each one cost.

          What Congress is trading

          Members of the US House must report every stock trade within 45 days under the STOCK Act. This tab reads those public filings from the House Clerk once a day and lines them up so you can see what was bought, what was sold, and where the money moved. It is a watch list, not a signal: a filing can arrive weeks after the trade.

          Public filings, not advice. Members report a range, not an exact amount, so every total here uses the middle of each range. Filings are self-reported, often late and sometimes amended. Price changes are estimates from daily closes. Senate filings are not included yet; they are at efdsearch.senate.gov.

          Investor knowledge

          Your paragraph

          Trade journal

          Record every paper trade here, one question at a time. The answers go into a CSV file on this laptop: date and time, ticker, timeframe, pattern, market regime and whether the trade is with or against it, breakout level and direction, measure-rule target, stop, exit price and date, and one line on what you learned.

          What each blank means
          Long or short
          Long means you buy expecting the price to rise. Short means you sell borrowed shares expecting it to fall, and buy them back later. A short can lose more than you put in.
          What the company sells
          One plain sentence. If you cannot write it, you do not know what you are buying, and a chart alone is not a reason.
          Pattern name
          The chart pattern as your pattern book names it, so you can look up its statistics instead of guessing them. The box suggests the common names.
          Breakout, date and level
          The day the price closed beyond the pattern's boundary, and that boundary price. Until a close breaks out there is no trade, only a watch.
          Bull or bear market
          The general market trend at the breakout, usually read from the S&P 500. The book reports its statistics separately for bull and bear markets because the results differ.
          With or against the market
          Filled in for you from the two choices above. Long in a bull market and short in a bear market are with the market; the other two are against it. Against is allowed, but the odds are usually worse.
          Break-even failure rate
          The share of this pattern's breakouts that failed to move even 5% in the breakout direction. Lower is better. It is the first number to check, before any target.
          Average move
          The average rise or decline after the breakout, measured to the ultimate high or low. Few trades capture all of it, so treat it as a ceiling, not a promise.
          Measure rule
          Take the height of the pattern, top to bottom, and add it to the breakout price for an upward breakout, or subtract it for a downward one. That is the target. The book also says how often the target was reached.
          Stop
          The close that proves the idea wrong. Decide it before you enter, never after. The distance from the level to the stop is the risk per share; the page computes it and shows the reward-to-risk ratio.
          Days to the ultimate high or low
          How long the average pattern took to reach its best price after the breakout. It says how patient to be, and when a trade that has gone nowhere has gone stale.
          Your failure rate and average move
          In the journal, a closed trade counts as a failure when the price moved less than 5% in the breakout direction by the exit, and the average move is the mean of those moves. That is a rough copy of how the book measures its patterns, using your exits instead of the ultimate high or low, so compare the two only after 20 to 30 trades on the same pattern.

          The failure rates, average moves, measure rule and days-to-ultimate tables are the statistics in Thomas Bulkowski's Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns. Look the numbers up for the exact pattern and market direction; do not reuse them from a different pattern.
